📖 科普朗读 · The Secret Life of Soil

Have you ever thought about what lies under your feet? Soil is much more than dirt. A handful of good soil is home to millions of living things, from tiny bacteria to busy earthworms. Soil forms slowly from broken rocks and dead plants. Over many years, wind, rain, and ice wear rocks into small pieces. Fallen leaves and animal remains mix with these pieces and turn into dark humus. Humus holds water and gives plants the nutrients they need. Worms and insects dig tunnels through the soil. Their movement loosens the earth, letting air and water reach plant roots. Bacteria and fungi break down dead material and recycle it into food for new life. This quiet teamwork keeps the whole soil healthy. Soil also does a big job for the planet. It cleans water as it moves through the ground and stores carbon from the air. Without healthy soil, forests would not grow and rivers would turn muddy. Next time you walk in a park, take a closer look at the ground. A spoonful of soil is a whole world of tiny workers, busy keeping life alive.

📖 科普朗读 · How Plants Make Food

Every green plant is a tiny food factory. Unlike animals, plants do not hunt for food. They make their own meals using sunlight, water, and air. This amazing process is called photosynthesis. The leaves are the main workshop. Inside each leaf are millions of tiny green cells. A green chemical called chlorophyll catches energy from sunlight. With this energy, the leaf joins water from the roots and carbon dioxide from the air together to make sugar. Plants use this sugar as food to grow, bloom, and produce fruit. Oxygen is released during the process, which is why fresh air feels so clean after a walk in the woods. Plants need the right conditions to work well. Too little light makes them grow weak and thin. Too much water can drown the roots. That is why gardeners choose sunny spots and water their plants carefully. Photosynthesis is not only important for plants. Almost every animal on Earth depends on it. Cows eat grass, and we eat the milk and meat from cows. In this way, the energy of the sun passes from plants to animals, and finally to our dinner plates.
